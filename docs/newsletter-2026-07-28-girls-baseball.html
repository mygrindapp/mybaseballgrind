Everything Happened In One Town, In One Week

Rockford, Illinois is where the Peaches played, the team most families know from the movie, part of a women's professional league that ran from 1943 to 1954 and then vanished for seventy years. Their home field was Beyer Stadium.

Here is what happened there in the last ten days. On July 18, Beyer Stadium reopened after a full renovation, new field, new dugouts, restored ticket booth, sitting inside a park now named for Maybelle Blair, who played in that league. The next morning, 800 players and coaches arrived for Baseball For All Nationals, the largest girls baseball tournament in the country, girls aged six to eighteen from across the US, Canada and Australia. And while they were still playing, the Women's Baseball World Cup opened across town, where Team USA went undefeated.

So a nine year old girl played a baseball game on the Rockford Peaches' restored home field, and could walk over and watch the US women's national team the same week. That has never been possible before in the history of this country. This issue is about what that means for your player, and exactly where she can go play.
 
What Is Happening Right Now

The World Cup. Group Rockford ran July 22 to 26. USA finished undefeated and won the group, closing it out against Hong Kong, China. USA, Australia and Canada punched their tickets to the 2027 Finals. A second group plays in Tainan from August 23 to 27 to fill the rest of the bracket, and the Finals come back to Rockford next July 19 to 25.

And this Saturday, something that has not existed since 1954. The Women's Pro Baseball League plays its first game on August 1 at Robin Roberts Stadium in Springfield, Illinois. Four teams: the Boston Hunters, New York Heights, San Francisco Firebells, and the Los Angeles Queens. Thirty games, seven innings each, playoffs in September. More than 500 players from 11 countries registered for the chance to be there.

Read that last part with your player in the room. For the first time in seventy years there is a professional baseball team an American girl can point at and say out loud that she wants to play there. One of them is in Los Angeles.
 
Where She Actually Plays: The Programs Doing The Work

This is the part most families never get told. There is a real network of girls baseball programs now, most of them nonprofits run by people who played and got pushed out themselves. If your player is the only girl on her team and starting to feel it, these exist so that she can spend a weekend being completely unremarkable.

Baseball For All

The hub of the whole thing, founded by Justine Siegal. BFA Nationals is the largest girls baseball tournament in the country, ages six to eighteen, and BFA also runs the Women's College Club Championship, which has doubled in size since it started in 2022. Siegal is also a co-founder of the new pro league, so the youth pipeline and the professional ceiling were built by the same person on purpose. If you only look at one thing on this list, look at this one.

LA Heat Girls' Baseball · our own backyard

A Los Angeles nonprofit started by two sisters, Emma and Mia Sites, as a service project. They run free baseball skills clinics for girls of all ages, coached by women, in and around LA, and they field teams for all-girl tournaments. They are careful to say they are not a travel team and not a league, and you do not have to join a team to come to a clinic. They just want girls to stay in baseball. For a Southern California family, this is the lowest possible barrier to your daughter standing on a field with a dozen other girls who play. Free, local, no tryout.

The Trailblazer Series · MLB and USA Baseball

Now in its ninth year, held every April at the Jackie Robinson Training Complex in Vero Beach, Florida, the weekend after Jackie Robinson Day. The 2026 event brought more than 90 girls from 23 states, three Canadian provinces and Panama, roughly ages 12 to 14, for games, instruction, evaluation and mentorship. MLB also runs the GRIT Girls Baseball Identification Tour, which is how a lot of players get seen and pulled into the development pipeline. These are application events, so the time to look is fall and winter, not April.

Regional clubs, if you travel

All-girls baseball clubs are spreading. Texas Twelve has been a nonprofit since 2006 and now runs out of six locations across Texas. The Florida Bolts, out of Tampa, field five teams from 8U to 18U. The San Francisco Bay Sox are the only all-girls travel baseball team in Northern California. Teams like the Chicago Pioneers have been winning national titles at BFA for years. None of this existed at scale when most of us were kids.

One honest note: coverage is uneven. Some regions have three options and some have none within a long drive. If your area is a hole, the free LA Heat style clinic model is the easiest thing in the world to copy, and BFA will talk to you about starting something.
 
The Part Of The History Nobody Taught Us

Here is the correction that reframes everything. Girls were never absent from baseball. They were removed from it.

Baseball was played by Americans of every gender from the 1830s, and women's colleges fielded real teams. As the sport professionalized, women were pushed off the diamonds, and the sporting goods magnate Albert Spalding formally declared baseball off limits to them. Softball came later, invented by men who wanted to keep playing indoors through winter. It was never designed as the girls' version of anything. It became that by default, and then it became the consolation prize.

Then in 1972 a 12 year old in Hoboken, New Jersey named Maria Pepe pitched three games for her Little League team. When word got out, Little League threatened to pull her team's charter and she was forced off the roster. The National Organization for Women took her case. In 1974 a New Jersey court ruled that Little League had to let girls play, and that June, Little League opened its doors.

Maria Pepe never got her season back. About fifty girls tried out in Hoboken alone the very next year. More than five million have played Little League baseball since. One 12 year old, three games, and a door that has never closed again.
 
The Youth Years (8 to 12): The Door Is Open, Then It Narrows

Through Little League age, a girl who wants to play baseball can play baseball. Most leagues are used to it now, and the ones that are not are usually one conversation away from being fine. This is the easy stretch, and it is worth saying plainly to your player: she is not a guest on that field. She is a player.

Then comes the squeeze, and it is structural rather than personal. Little League gives girls a place through about age 12. After that the baseball options thin out fast while the softball options multiply, partly because when schools were told to add opportunities for girls, softball got designated as the equivalent to baseball. So around 12 or 13 a family hears the same sentence from a well meaning coach: "She should probably switch to softball now."

That sentence is usually offered as practical advice, not as an insult, and sometimes softball genuinely is the better path for a kid who wants it. But it should be a choice your player makes, not a default she gets handed. Baseball and softball are different sports. Different ball, different distances, different pitch. A girl who loves baseball is not a softball player who has not converted yet. And the programs above exist precisely so that 12 is no longer the end of the road.
 
High School And College: Small Numbers, Real Doors

High school. I want to give you the honest number rather than a slogan. No state sponsors girls high school baseball as its own sport, and in 2024-25, 1,381 girls played on high school baseball teams nationally. That is small. It is also 1,381 real girls who made rosters, and it is a number that only exists because each of them tried. If this is your player's path, expect her to be the only girl in the program, and know that the two things that help most are telling the coach early and directly what she wants, and having a record of her work that speaks before anyone's opinion does.

College. The NCAA does not offer women's baseball, so there is no scholarship path today. What does exist, and what almost no family knows about, is women's college club baseball, with a national championship run by Baseball For All that has doubled in size since 2022. It is club baseball. It is also real baseball at the college level, played by women, and it did not exist at all a few years ago.

And now above all of it sits a professional league. The ladder is not complete and I am not going to pretend it is. But for the first time it goes all the way up.
 
Coach's Take

In 35 years I have coached a lot of girls on baseball fields. I have never once had to coach one differently. What I have watched, more times than I want to admit, is a girl get quietly talked out of a game she loved by people who were not being cruel and thought they were being realistic. The realism was just out of date. It is more out of date this week than it was last week.

Here is the part that matters for a parent. The girls I saw make it through that 12 to 14 year old squeeze were almost never the biggest or the hardest throwing. They were the ones who could show, not argue. When a kid can say what she has been working on, for how long, and what changed, she stops being a question a coach has to decide about and becomes a player he has to evaluate. That is a much better position to be in, and it is available to a 12 year old right now.
