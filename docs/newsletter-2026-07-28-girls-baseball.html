This Week
Rockford, Illinois. Again.

Two days ago, in Rockford, Illinois, Team USA finished an undefeated run through their group at the Women's Baseball World Cup. Not softball. Baseball. Same ball, same 90 foot bases, same overhand pitching.

Rockford is not a random host city. Rockford is where the Peaches played, the team most families know from the movie, part of a women's professional league that ran from 1943 to 1954 and then disappeared for seventy years. Women's international baseball came back to that exact town this month, and the World Cup Finals return there next July.

If you have a daughter who loves baseball and you have been quietly wondering how long she gets to keep playing it, this issue is for you. The answer changed while a lot of us were not looking.
 
What Is Happening Right Now

The World Cup. Group Rockford ran July 22 to 26. USA went undefeated and finished on top, closing it out against Hong Kong, China. USA, Australia and Canada punched tickets to the 2027 Finals. The second group plays in Tainan from August 23 to 27 to fill the rest of the bracket, and the Finals come back to Rockford next July 19 to 25.

And this Saturday, something that has not existed since 1954. The Women's Pro Baseball League plays its first game on August 1 at Robin Roberts Stadium in Springfield, Illinois. Four teams: the Boston Hunters, New York Heights, San Francisco Firebells, and the Los Angeles Queens. Thirty games, seven innings each, playoffs in September. More than 500 players from 11 countries registered for the chance.

Read that last part again with your player in the room. For the first time in seventy years, there is a professional baseball team an American girl can point at and say out loud that she wants to play there. One of them is in Los Angeles.
 
The Part Of The History Nobody Taught Us

Here is the correction that reframes everything. Girls were never absent from baseball. They were removed from it.

Baseball was played by Americans of every gender since the 1830s, and women's colleges fielded real teams. As the sport professionalized, women were pushed off the diamonds, and sporting goods magnate Albert Spalding formally declared baseball off limits to them. Softball came later, invented by men who wanted to keep playing indoors through winter. It was never designed as the girls' version of anything. It became that by default, and it became the consolation prize.

Then in 1972 a 12 year old in Hoboken, New Jersey named Maria Pepe pitched three games for her Little League team. When word got out, Little League threatened to pull her team's charter, and she was forced off the roster. The National Organization for Women took her case. In 1974 a New Jersey court ruled that Little League had to let girls play, and that June, Little League opened its doors.

Maria Pepe never got her season back. About fifty girls tried out in Hoboken alone the very next year. More than five million have played Little League baseball since. One 12 year old, three games, and a door that has never closed again.
 
The Youth Years (8 to 12): The Door Is Open, And Then It Narrows

Through Little League age, a girl who wants to play baseball can play baseball. Most leagues are used to it now, and the ones who are not are usually one conversation away from being fine. This is the easy stretch, and it is worth saying plainly to your player: she is not a guest on that field. She is a player.

Then comes the squeeze, and it is structural rather than personal. Little League gives girls a place through about age 12. After that the baseball options thin out fast while the softball options multiply, partly because when schools were told to add opportunities for girls, softball got designated as the equivalent to baseball. So around 12 or 13 a family hears the same sentence from a well meaning coach: "She should probably switch to softball now."

That sentence is usually offered as practical advice, not as an insult, and sometimes softball genuinely is the better path for a kid who wants it. But it should be a choice your player makes, not a default she gets handed. Baseball and softball are different sports. Different ball, different distances, different pitch. A girl who loves baseball is not a softball player who has not converted yet.
 
High School And College: The Doors That Just Opened

High school. Girls can and do try out for high school baseball teams, and more of them make rosters every year. The honest version: she may be the only girl in the program, and that takes something. It also builds something. If this is your player's path, the two things that help most are a coach who is told early and directly what she wants, and a record of her own work that speaks for her before anyone's opinion does.

College. This is the newest door and most families do not know it exists. Women's college club baseball is growing, and Baseball For All runs a Women's College Club Championship that has doubled in size since it started in 2022. It is club rather than NCAA scholarship baseball today, but it is real baseball at the college level, and it did not exist at all when most of us were kids.

Under all of it sits Baseball For All, founded by Justine Siegal, which runs the largest girls baseball tournament in the country and is where a lot of girls first look around and see a whole field of players who look like them. Siegal is also a co-founder of the new pro league. The youth pipeline and the pro league were built by the same person, on purpose.
 
Coach's Take

In 35 years I have coached a lot of girls on baseball fields. I have never once had to coach one differently. What I have watched, more times than I want to admit, is a girl get quietly talked out of a game she loved, by people who were not being cruel and thought they were being realistic. The realism was just out of date.

Here is the part that matters for a parent. The girls I saw make it through that 12 to 14 year old squeeze were almost never the biggest or the hardest throwing. They were the ones who could show, not argue. When a kid can say what she has been working on, for how long, and what changed, she stops being a question a coach has to decide about and becomes a player he has to evaluate. That is a much better position to be in, and it is available to a 12 year old.

That is a lot of what the log is for. Every rep, every note, every honest grade of a day, sitting in one place. It is not proof that anyone owes her a roster spot. It is proof she has been doing the work all along, which is the only argument that has ever moved a coach anyway.
