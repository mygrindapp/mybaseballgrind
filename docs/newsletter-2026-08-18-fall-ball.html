We Called It The Lab

Saturday I was at Dodger Stadium watching Reggie Smith honored as a Legend of Dodger Baseball. I have known him about 25 years. I do not think I watched one full inning.

I spent the night with the past players and his family, watching people come through one after another to pay their respects. What I kept thinking about was the years that built me into the coach I am now. All of us cage rats. Back when baseball was truly about development.

We called it the lab. Nobody kept score in the lab. Nobody asked what our record was. The whole point was the work. And the work is what every person in that stadium on Saturday was actually there for, decades later.

It was probably one of the best times I have ever had. I am telling you about it in August for a reason, because this is the exact week your player starts getting measured on the same thing.
 
Fall Ball Is Not A Tryout. But You Are Being Evaluated.

There is no cut list in October, and no coach worth playing for is deciding a player's future off a fall scrimmage. But do not confuse that with nobody watching. Coaches are evaluating right now, and we are building spring rosters off what we see this fall.

We are just not scoring what your player thinks we are scoring. I am a high school head coach. Here is how it actually sounds in the coaches' room in November, and I promise you nobody says "he hit .420 in the fall."

"He is the first one in every day." That sentence has decided more roster spots than any swing I have seen. It costs nothing and it cannot be faked over eight weeks.
"I can put him anywhere." Versatility is the cheapest way for a borderline player to become undroppable.
"He is good, but." That one should scare you, and it is almost never about ability. It is the eye-roll after a correction and how he talked to the kid who booted one behind him.

Three of those have nothing to do with performance. That is not softness, it is practical. I am choosing who I have to spend February through May with, on a bus, in the rain, after a loss.

Tell your player this part, because it is good news: the fall evaluation is not a talent contest. It is an effort and character contest. Any player can win that one, starting the day they decide to.

The Wins This Fall Happen In The Gym

Winning and losing games this time of year does not matter. The wins you bank in the weight room do. Fall is the best strength window on the calendar: light competitive load, thin schedule, and a long enough runway before spring for real adaptation. The work done now shows up in March, when it counts.

Youth. Not a barbell conversation. This is the age for movement: sprinting, jumping, climbing, throwing things that are not baseballs. And it is the season to try the position they are curious about, because in April nobody has time to experiment. Give them one goal for the whole fall. One.

High school. Two or three quality sessions a week for fourteen weeks beats five brutal weeks and then nothing. Train movements, not muscles. Write the numbers down. And eat, because you cannot build tissue out of nothing, and this is where it catches most of them.

College age and college bound. School is back and schedules are still settling. This is the short window where a counselor can still fix one, and it closes fast. Every core course grade this term goes onto the NCAA transcript, and the sliding scale does not care that it was a busy fall.

Grades close doors that talent does not reopen. I have watched it happen to better players than most of us ever coached.
 
Shut The Arm Down. Do Not Shut The Work Down.

This is the one I will not bend on. If your player pitched through spring and summer, the throwing arm needs a genuine shutdown. Two to three months with no competitive throwing. That is not overcautious, it is the difference between an arm that lasts until 18 and one that does not.

But shutting the arm down is not shutting the player down. Still in the weight room. Still at practice. Still playing a position, still hitting, still around their teammates. The rest is for the throwing arm, not for the athlete.

One more thing about the calendar. Fall seasons fail in weeks three through five. The novelty is gone, school piles up, the weather turns, and the games do not matter. That is the exact stretch coaches watch hardest. Nobody is impressed by week one effort. Everybody notices week four effort. Say that to your player out loud, because if they know the test is coming, they tend to pass it.

This Week's Journal Prompts

For your player: "What is the one thing I am going to be better at by the end of this fall? And what did I do about it today?" Same two questions, every entry, for eight weeks. That is the whole program.

For you: "When my player fails in front of me, what does my face do?" That answer decides whether your house is a lab or an audition. It is not the pep talk afterward. It is the first two seconds.
