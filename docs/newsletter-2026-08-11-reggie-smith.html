Something Bigger Than Tryouts Is Happening Saturday

I promised you the tryout issue this week. It is written, and it is coming next Tuesday. But this Saturday at Dodger Stadium, a man named Reggie Smith becomes the 10th Legend of Dodger Baseball, and I could not let that happen without telling you who he is.

The scoreboard will handle his résumé. Seventeen big-league seasons. Seven All-Star games. Four World Series and a ring with the 1981 Dodgers. And next month the Negro Leagues Baseball Museum honors him too.

Here is what the scoreboard cannot tell you: I have spent almost 25 years standing next to him. And the two lessons he taught me are the two I hand your player every week in this newsletter.
 
The Day We Got Run Off His Field

My son was nine. We had just moved near Encino, and we were always hunting for a field to get a workout in. One day we found one: quiet, empty, a few trailers off to the side, one with a big red R painted on it. We helped ourselves to some ground balls.

That is when Reggie Smith himself walked out and told us, firm but fair, that we could not be on that field without permission. We packed up, a little shook, because we had just been run off a ballfield by a seven-time All-Star.

A week later my son said, "Dad, he told us we just need to ask." So we went back and asked. What happened next, the 5:30 AM invitation, the year I spent throwing batting practice to earn my way in, and how a dad and his boy became part of the Encino family, is the story I finally wrote down this week.

Lesson One: Attitude And Aptitude

The first real lesson Reggie taught me is the one I repeat most. What coaches look for, above almost everything else, is attitude and aptitude. Those two get graded higher than half the tools a player walks in the door with. You can find a kid who runs fast and throws hard. What is rare is the kid who wants it, listens, and turns instruction into work.

Youth. This is the age where attitude IS the skill. Nobody is measuring exit velocity at nine. The player who listens to the coach and tries the change on the very next rep is already ahead, and that habit is teachable at the dinner table.

High school. Tryout season is here, and I will tell you next week exactly how this plays out on the field. The short version: most close roster calls are decided by coachability, not tools, because a coach is choosing who they get to spend February with.

College age. Recruiters call it makeup, and they call the summer coach, the high school coach, and the guidance office to check it. By this level the tools all look similar. The file that separates players is the one Reggie named 25 years ago.
 
Lesson Two: It Is A Failure Sport

Reggie also taught me not to fear failure, because baseball and softball are failure sports. Get a hit three times out of ten and you are a success. Which means your player can do everything right and still walk back to the dugout seven times out of ten.

So the real skill is what happens after the out. Keep the work ethic. Keep your composure. Step back in the box. Reggie lived that through four World Series and taught it as plainly as he taught the swing.

And his last piece never changes: stay humble, stay respectful, and never forget to have fun. The players who last hold onto all three. That is true at nine, at sixteen, and in a college dugout.

This Week's Journal Prompts

For your player: "The last time a coach told me to change something, did I change it on the next rep? What did I do after my last bad at bat?" Honest answers only. That page is where attitude gets built.

For you: "Who is the Reggie in my player's life, the teacher who tells them the truth? And have they earned their way in with that person, or am I trying to buy the shortcut?" Twenty-five years later I can tell you: the year of batting practice was the gift.
