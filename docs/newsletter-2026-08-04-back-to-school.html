One Week Until The Bell

Summer ball is done. Around here school starts next Tuesday, and most of the country is inside the same two weeks.

This is the quietest stretch of the whole year, and it is also the one families waste most often. The season is over so nobody is thinking about baseball, and school has not started so nobody is thinking about school either. Seven days of nothing, and then everything at once.

So here are three questions worth asking this week, while there is still time to do something about the answers. Did the body actually rest. Did they get in the weight room. And are they in the right classes.
 
Question One: Did The Arm And The Body Actually Rest?

Be honest about this one, because the answer for most families is no. Summer ended, fall ball sign-ups opened, and somewhere in there the "break" turned into lessons twice a week and a bucket of balls in the driveway.

A throwing arm needs real time off. Not light throwing. Off. The medical guidance most of us follow is a few months a year where a player does not throw competitively at all, and at least a few weeks where they do not throw much of anything. If your player threw all summer and starts fall ball in three weeks, that window is right now and it is the only one they get.

Younger players, roughly 10 to 14. This is where the damage compounds quietly. They do not complain because it does not hurt yet, and they want to play. Rest is a decision you make for them, not one they will ask for.

High school. Tryouts and fall workouts are close enough now that people panic and ramp up too fast. The right move is a real down week, then a build. Not a sprint from zero.

College age. Fall is a grind and the calendar barely lets up, which makes deliberate down time something they have to schedule on purpose or it never happens.
 
Question Two: Did They Get In The Weight Room?

Here is the thing nobody tells families in August. The gains that show up next spring are being made right now, in the eight weeks when there are no games. Once the season starts you are maintaining, not building.

And "weight room" means different things at different ages.

Youth. No barbell required and honestly not wanted yet. Push-ups, squats, lunges, planks, sprints, and learning to move well. Bodyweight strength and athleticism carry a 12 year old further than anything on a rack.

High school. This is the window. Most high schools run an offseason lifting period, often as a class, and the players who show up in August are the ones who look different in February. If your school offers it and your player is not in it, that is a conversation to have this week, before schedules lock.

College age. The program tells them what to do. The job is doing it, and eating enough to make it count.

One caution from 35 years of watching this go wrong: strength work is only useful on a body that is recovered. If the answer to question one was no, fix that first. You cannot build on a tired arm.
 
Question Three: Right Classes, Right Grades

This is the one that ends more baseball careers than any injury, and it is the easiest to fix in the first week of school instead of the last.

Check the schedule before it locks. The first week is when a counselor will still move a class without a fight. If your player is a freshman or sophomore, make sure the core courses are actually on there. If they are a junior, this is the year that matters most.

Know which classes even count. The NCAA does not use your school's GPA. It builds its own from a specific list of core courses, unweighted, with electives thrown out. That is how a 3.7 on the report card comes back closer to 2.9. Every high school files an approved course list, so a class only counts if your school filed it that way.

And know the deadline nobody mentions. For Division I, ten of the sixteen core courses have to be done before senior year starts, and once it starts those ten are locked. You cannot retake your way out of it as a senior. The window to fix a weak core grade is junior year and earlier.

If your player is younger, none of this is urgent yet, but the habit is. A sixth grader who knows grades are part of the deal never has to be told at sixteen.

What Is Worth Having For The School Year

Once school starts, the free hours disappear. What survives the school year is the stuff that lets a player get real work in at home in twenty minutes. Short list, all things I actually use or recommend.

Arm care bands

The single highest-value thing on this list, and the cheapest. Five minutes before throwing, five minutes after. If your player does one new thing this school year, make it this.

A tee and a net

The whole point is removing the excuse. A garage setup means forty swings happen on a Tuesday night in January when driving to a cage never would.

A resistance band set

For the players who do not have a school weight room yet, or who need something on the road. Enough to keep a base through the winter.

A reaction ball

Ten dollars, fits in a backpack, and it trains the first step and the hands better than most drills that take a field and a coach.

One book

For a high schooler, The Mental Game of Baseball. For a parent, The Arm, which will change how you think about pitch counts for good.

This Week's Journal Prompts

For your player: "What is one thing I want to be better at by the time the season starts, and what am I doing about it this month?" Write it down now. Read it in February.

For you: "Did my player actually get a break this summer, or did I just call it one?" Answer that honestly before you sign anything for fall.

None of this week's three questions takes long to answer. A rest week is a decision. A schedule change is one conversation with a counselor. A lifting class is a box on a form. But all three of them get harder every day school is in session, and by October they are next year's problem.

So take the quiet week. Ask the three questions out loud, at dinner, with your player in the room. Then write the answers down somewhere you will actually see them again.
