Right On Schedule

I owe you two Tuesdays. School started, I took over a high school program, and the first weeks of a season eat everything around them. I tell your player to get the rep in even when the week wins. This week I am taking my own advice.

Because three weeks ago I told you something in this newsletter. I said rosters would settle, playing time would get uneven, and right about now a lot of parents would send a text they regret.

Right on schedule, they have started landing in my phone. So let me keep the promise. Here is how those messages read from the coach's chair, what actually works, and the one question that gets a real answer every time.
 
The 9:47 PM Text, From The Other Side

Every coach knows the message. It arrives the night of a game, it is longer than it needs to be, and somewhere in it is a sentence comparing your kid to somebody else's kid. I have read hundreds of them over 35 years. I can tell you exactly what happens on my end: I stop reading at the comparison.

Not because I do not care. Because the message is not answerable. I cannot discuss another family's player with you. And a coach who just spent four hours running a game has nothing good left at 9:47 PM. Neither do you. That is why the text gets regretted.

The rules that actually work, from the receiving end:
Wait 24 hours. Every program I respect has this rule and every experienced parent already follows it. Nothing about playing time is an emergency.
Never open with minutes. "Why isn't my player playing" has no good answer. "What does my player need to do to earn more" has nothing but good answers. Same concern, opposite outcome.
Ask for a conversation, not a verdict. Email beats text. A scheduled ten minutes beats the parking lot every single time. The parking lot postgame is where relationships with coaches go to die.

And the question I promised you, the one that gets a real answer from every coach I have ever known: "What should my player be working on that they are not?" That question cannot offend anybody. It hands the coach the job they actually signed up for, which is coaching. You will learn more from the answer than from a season of bleacher theories.

Who Has The Conversation Changes With Age

Youth. This is your conversation to have, and remember who you are having it with. Most youth coaches are volunteers with a day job and a kid on the roster. Assume good faith. Open by offering help before you ask for anything, because the coach drowning in eleven other kids remembers the parent who showed up with hands, not notes.

High school. The conversation belongs to your player now. I will tell you something from inside the coaches' room: when a player walks in on their own and asks what they need to work on, their stock goes up before I answer the question. When a parent asks it for them, it goes the other way. Not because the parent did something wrong. Because the sport at this level is asking who owns the work.

College age and college bound. Parents are out of the conversation entirely. A college coach will not take the call, and making it can genuinely hurt your player. Which means the skill has to be built before they leave. The sophomore who practices asking their high school coach hard questions is doing recruiting prep nobody posts about.

Teach it the same way you taught them to order their own food. Awkward at 12, automatic at 17, and the whole point was never the hamburger.
 
When You Should Step In

None of this means parents go silent. There is a short list where you skip the 24 hours, skip the player-led version, and go straight to the coach as an adult protecting a kid.

Health and safety, always. A pitcher being run out there past any sane workload. A player told to hide an injury. Anything touching a doctor's note. That is not a playing time conversation and you should not treat it like one.
Money and logistics, yours. Fees, travel, schedules that got promised and moved. Business questions belong to the person paying.
How your kid is treated as a person. Coaching hard is fine. Demeaning a kid is not, and you do not need a rule from a newsletter to know the difference when you see it.

Everything else, the lineup, the batting order, the position, the innings, runs through the player and the one question. Save your capital for the short list. Parents who spend it there get listened to when it matters.

This Week's Journal Prompts

For your player: "What did my coach ask me to work on, in their exact words? What did I do about it today?" Write the answer the day they say it. A coach who sees their own words coming back as finished work will move that player up every time.

For you: "Would I be comfortable if what I am about to send were read out loud at the team meeting?" If yes, send it. If no, you already know.
