Can Girls Play Baseball? A Coach's Guide to Every Level She Can Play

By Coach Mike Young · 35 years coaching youth baseball & softball · July 27, 2026 · 11 min read

Every season a parent asks me some version of the same question, usually quietly, usually after practice. She loves baseball. How long does she actually get to play it? For a long time the honest answer was complicated. In 2026 it is not. This is the guide I wish those parents had been handed years ago.

The short answer

Yes. Girls can play baseball, at every level, and nothing requires your daughter to switch to softball. Little League has been open to girls since June 1974. Girls try out for and make high school baseball teams. Women play college club baseball with a national championship. There is a USA women's national team that competes in a World Cup. And as of August 1, 2026, there is a professional women's baseball league in the United States for the first time since 1954.

What she runs into is not a rule. It is a gap in available teams that opens around age 12, plus a lot of well-meaning adults whose information is out of date. Both of those are solvable, and the rest of this guide is how.

The proof, from one week in one town

If you want a single snapshot of where girls baseball actually is, look at what happened in Rockford, Illinois in July 2026.

Rockford is where the Rockford Peaches played, the team most families know from the movie, part of the All-American Girls Professional Baseball League that ran from 1943 to 1954. Their home field was Beyer Stadium. On July 18, 2026, that ballpark reopened after a full renovation, inside a park now named for Maybelle Blair, who played in that league. The next morning, roughly 800 players and coaches arrived for Baseball For All Nationals, the largest girls baseball tournament in the country, girls aged six to eighteen from across the United States, Canada and Australia. And while they were still playing, the WBSC Women's Baseball World Cup opened across the same town, where Team USA went undefeated and won its group.

A nine-year-old girl played a tournament game on the Rockford Peaches' restored home field and could walk across town to watch the United States women's national team the same week. That combination has not happened before, and the World Cup Finals return to Rockford in July 2027.

Where she plays right now: the programs

This is the part most families are never told. There is a real network of girls baseball organizations, most of them nonprofits, many founded by women who played and got pushed out themselves.

Baseball For All

The hub of the whole thing, founded by Justine Siegal. Baseball For All runs BFA Nationals, the largest girls baseball tournament in the country, for ages six to eighteen. It also runs a Women's College Club Championship that has doubled in size since it began in 2022. Siegal went on to co-found the new professional league, so the youth pipeline and the professional ceiling were deliberately built by the same person. Baseball For All publishes a directory of girls baseball teams, which is the fastest way to find out what exists near you.

Regional all-girls clubs

All-girls baseball clubs are spreading, and several have been running for years:

  • Texas Twelve has been a nonprofit since 2006 and operates out of six locations across Texas.
  • The Florida Bolts, based in Tampa, field five teams covering ages 8U through 18U.
  • The San Francisco Bay Sox are the only all-girls travel baseball team in Northern California.
  • LA Heat Girls' Baseball, a Los Angeles nonprofit started by two sisters, runs free baseball skills clinics for girls of all ages, coached by women, and fields teams for all-girls tournaments. You do not have to join a team to attend a clinic.
  • The Chicago Pioneers have been winning national titles at BFA for years.

Coverage is still filling in and new clubs start every year. If nothing exists near you, the free clinic model is genuinely easy to copy, and Baseball For All will talk to any parent who wants to start something.

MLB and USA Baseball

The Trailblazer Series is Major League Baseball's girls baseball development event, run with USA Baseball. It is now in its ninth year and is held each April at the Jackie Robinson Training Complex in Vero Beach, Florida, the weekend after Jackie Robinson Day. The 2026 event brought more than 90 girls from 23 states, three Canadian provinces and Panama, roughly ages 12 to 14, for games, instruction, evaluation and mentorship. MLB also runs the GRIT Girls Baseball Identification Tour, which feeds the development pipeline. These are application events, so the time to look is fall and winter, not the week before.

The youth years, ages 8 to 12

Through Little League age, a girl who wants to play baseball can play baseball. Most leagues are used to it now, and the ones that are not are usually one conversation away from being fine. Say it plainly to your player: she is not a guest on that field, she is a player on that team.

This is also the stretch where a habit is worth more than a growth spurt. The girls I have watched come through the next few years intact were rarely the biggest or hardest throwing at 11. They were the ones who could show what they had been working on instead of arguing about it.

The age-12 squeeze, and why it happens

Around 12 or 13, a lot of families hear the same sentence from a well-meaning coach: she should probably switch to softball now.

Here is what is actually behind it. Little League gives girls a guaranteed place through about age 12. After that the local baseball options thin out quickly while softball options multiply, in large part because when schools were required to expand opportunities for girls, softball was designated as the equivalent to baseball. So the squeeze is real, but it is a shortage of nearby teams, not a rule and not a judgment about your daughter.

The answer is to know that the programs above exist. Twelve is where the local road narrows. It is not where the sport ends.

Girls baseball vs softball: they are different sports

This trips up more families than anything else, so let me be precise. Baseball and softball differ in ball size, basepath length, pitching distance, and the pitching motion itself, overhand versus the underhand windmill. They are related sports, not male and female versions of one sport.

The history is worth knowing because it explains the assumption. Americans of every gender played baseball from the 1830s on, and women's colleges fielded real teams. As the sport professionalized, women were pushed off the diamonds, and sporting goods magnate Albert Spalding formally declared baseball off limits to them. Softball came later, invented by men who wanted to keep playing indoors through winter. It was never designed as the girls' version of anything. Institutions assigned it that role. Players did not.

None of that is a knock on softball. It is a great game, I have coached it for decades, and plenty of girls choose it on purpose and never look back. The problem was never softball. It was the assumption that every girl belonged in it and no girl belonged in baseball. If your daughter wants to play softball, that is a good choice. It should just be her choice.

High school baseball

Girls do play high school baseball. In the 2024-25 school year, 1,381 girls played on high school baseball teams in the United States, according to the National Federation of State High School Associations, and they did it without a single state sponsoring girls baseball as its own sport. They tried out for the school baseball team and made it.

Expect her to be the only girl in the program, and expect that to take something. Two things help more than anything else. First, tell the coach early and directly what she wants, before tryouts, not during. Second, arrive with a record. When a player can say exactly what she has been working on, for how long, and what changed, she stops being a question the coach has to decide about and becomes a player he or she has to evaluate. That is a far better position, and it is available to a 12-year-old.

College baseball for women

This is the newest door and almost no family knows it is there. The NCAA does not offer women's baseball, so there is no scholarship path today. What does exist is women's college club baseball, with a national championship run by Baseball For All that has doubled in size since it started in 2022. Four years, twice as many programs. Every one of those teams exists because women showed up and built it, and the trend is pointing in one direction.

Professional baseball

On August 1, 2026, the Women's Pro Baseball League plays its first game at Robin Roberts Stadium in Springfield, Illinois. Four teams: the Boston Hunters, New York Heights, San Francisco Firebells, and the Los Angeles Queens. Thirty games, seven innings each, playoffs in September. More than 500 players from 11 countries registered for a shot at the rosters.

It is the first women's professional baseball league in this country since the AAGPBL folded in 1954. For the first time in seventy years, an American girl can point at a professional baseball team and say she wants to play there.

Put the whole thing end to end and the ladder now reads: free local clinic, all-girls travel club, national tournament, high school roster, college club, Team USA, paycheck. Every rung was built in the last few years, and they are still building.

What parents can actually do

  • Find the nearest program and put one date on the calendar, even if it is a free clinic two months out. A weekend where she is not the only girl on the field changes how the rest of the season feels.
  • Tell her the calendar out loud, with the team names in it. Kids decide what is possible based on what they have seen.
  • Talk to the coach before tryouts, not after cuts.
  • Let her keep her own record. What she worked on, for how long, what changed. It is the only argument that has ever moved a coach.
  • Keep the softball door open without pushing her through it. Skills transfer both ways, and a player who switches by choice at 15 has lost nothing.

Common questions from parents

Can girls play Little League baseball? Yes, and they have been able to since June 1974, after 12-year-old Maria Pepe was forced off her Hoboken, New Jersey team in 1972 and the National Organization for Women took the case. A New Jersey court ruled in 1974 that Little League had to let girls play. About fifty girls tried out in Hoboken alone the following year, and more than five million have played Little League baseball since.

Will playing baseball hurt her chances at a softball scholarship? Not if the switch happens with runway. Skills transfer, and the fielding, throwing and approach carry over. What does not transfer well is a rushed conversion at 17. If softball scholarships are the goal, plan the transition deliberately rather than treating age 12 as an automatic deadline.

She is the only girl on the team and it is getting to her. What helps? One weekend at an all-girls event, more than anything I have seen. It is not about competition level. It is about the relief of not being the exception for two days. That is the entire reason organizations like Baseball For All and LA Heat exist.

Is women's baseball actually growing, or is this a moment? The measurable things are all up: a professional league launched in 2026, the college club championship doubled in four years, the Trailblazer Series is in its ninth year drawing players from three countries, and the World Cup Finals come to Rockford in 2027. That is infrastructure, not a news cycle.

The bottom line

If your daughter loves baseball, this is the best moment there has ever been to be her. Not the easiest, the best. She is arriving exactly as the game opens up, and the people building it are building it for her.

Your job is smaller than it feels. Find her one program, tell her the truth about how far this goes, and help her keep a record of the work. The game will handle the rest.
