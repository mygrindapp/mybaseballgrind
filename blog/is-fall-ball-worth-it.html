Is Fall Ball Worth It? Your Evaluation Already Started

By Coach Mike Young · 35 years coaching youth baseball & softball · August 17, 2026 · 14 min read

Rosters are forming, the weight room is open, and school is back. Here is what is actually being measured this fall.

The short answer: fall ball is worth it, but not for the games. Nobody is keeping the October standings. Coaches are evaluating work ethic and character and filling spring rosters off fall progressions, and the strength work your player does now is what shows up in March. Play it for the wins in the gym, not the ones on the scoreboard.

This past Saturday I was at Dodger Stadium watching Reggie Smith honored as a Dodger legend. I have known him about 25 years. I do not think I watched one full inning.

I spent the night with the past players and his family, watching people come through one after another to pay their respects. And what I kept thinking about was the years that built me into the coach I am now. All of us cage rats. Back when baseball was truly about development.

We called it the lab.

Nobody kept score in the lab. Nobody asked what our record was. The whole point was the work. And the work is what every single person in that stadium on Saturday was actually there for, decades later.

I am telling you that in August for a reason. Right now the fall registration email is sitting in your inbox, your player said sure, whatever, and you are the one holding a decision that costs money, weekends and gas. After 35 years coaching baseball and softball, here is what I wish more families understood about these particular eight weeks: the games are the least important part of them. And the part that matters most is already being watched.

You are being evaluated. Just not on what you think.

Let me say the honest version first, because the soft version does nobody any good.

Fall is not a tryout. There is no cut list in October and no coach worth playing for is deciding a player's future off a fall scrimmage. But do not confuse that with nothing being watched. Coaches are absolutely evaluating right now, and they are building their spring rosters off what they see this fall.

They are just not scoring what your player thinks they are scoring.

Here is what a coach is actually writing down, whether or not there is a clipboard in their hand:

  • Who shows up to the weight room every time. Not most times. Every time. This is the loudest signal a player sends all fall and it is entirely within their control.
  • Character on a bad day. Anybody is a good teammate after three hits. The evaluation is what they look like after an 0 for 4, after a bad call, after somebody else got the position they wanted.
  • Who picks up the equipment. Every coach reading this just nodded. It sounds small. It is not small. It tells us exactly who is in it for themselves.
  • Progression, not position. Nobody is being graded on where they were in week one. They are being graded on the distance between week one and week eight.

Tell your player this plainly, because it is genuinely good news: the fall evaluation is not a talent contest. It is an effort and character contest. Those are things any player can win, starting the day they decide to.

What actually gets said in the coaches' room

I am a high school head coach. Let me tell you how these conversations really sound in November, because it is not what parents picture.

Nobody says "he hit .420 in the fall." Not once. What gets said is closer to this:

  • "He is the first one in every day." That sentence has decided more roster spots than any swing I have ever seen. It costs nothing and it cannot be faked over eight weeks.
  • "I can put him anywhere." Versatility is the cheapest way for a borderline player to become undroppable. The kid who caught a few innings, took some reps at third and can run to the outfield in an emergency is on the bus. The one who only plays one spot has to be clearly better at it than everybody else.
  • "He is good, but." This is the one that should scare you, and it is almost never about ability. It is about the eye-roll after a correction, the bat flip in a meaningless game, the way he talked to a teammate who booted one behind him. When a coach has to say "but," a lot of talent gets left in the parking lot.
  • "Did anybody check his grades?" Asked early, every year, and it has ended things before the season ever started.

Notice that three of those four have nothing to do with how the player performs. That is not softness, it is practical. A coach is choosing who they have to spend February through May with, on a bus, in the rain, after a loss. Talent that makes that harder is not worth what it costs.

The wins this fall happen in the gym

If your player takes one thing out of this season, make it this: winning and losing games in October does not matter. The wins you bank in the weight room do.

Fall is the single best strength window on the calendar. The competitive load is light, the schedule is thin, and there is a long enough runway before spring for real adaptation to happen. A player who trains seriously from September through December walks into February bigger, stronger and more durable than the version of themselves who spent the fall chasing a trophy that nobody will remember by Thanksgiving.

That is not a small edge. That is often the entire difference between the player who makes the jump and the one who plateaus.

So keep the strength work going through the season, not around it. Two to three quality sessions a week, consistently, beats five sessions for two weeks and then nothing. And measure it. A player who can point to what their numbers were in September and what they are in December has proof of something real, and proof changes how a kid sees themselves.

What a fall strength block actually looks like

I am not going to hand you a program off a blog post, and you should be suspicious of anybody who does. Age, training history and physical maturity change everything, and a qualified strength coach is worth the money. But the principles are not a secret, and most families are getting them wrong in the same three ways.

  • Consistency beats intensity, every time. Two or three quality sessions a week for fourteen weeks will out-produce five brutal sessions a week for three weeks followed by nothing. The plan your player will actually do is better than the perfect plan they will quit.
  • Train movements, not muscles. Push, pull, squat, hinge, carry, rotate. A young athlete does not need an arm day. They need to get strong in the patterns the sport actually uses, and then they need to learn to move that strength fast.
  • Write down the numbers. This is the part almost everybody skips and it is the part that changes how a kid sees themselves. A player who can look at September and look at December and see the difference in black and white is a different athlete in the spring. Not because the numbers went up. Because they now believe work produces results, and that belief transfers to everything.
  • Feed it. You cannot build tissue out of nothing. A teenager adding strength while eating like they are still eleven is spinning wheels, and the fall is when this catches up to most of them.

The point is not to turn your player into a weight room athlete. It is that the fall is the only long, uninterrupted block on the calendar where getting stronger is the primary job instead of an afterthought squeezed between tournaments.

Shut the arm down. Do not shut the work down.

This is the one place I will not bend, and it is also the place families get the advice backwards.

If your player pitched through spring and summer, their throwing arm needs a genuine shutdown period. Two to three months with no competitive throwing. That is not optional and it is not overcautious. It is the difference between an arm that lasts until 18 and one that does not. The full picture is in youth pitcher arm care.

But shutting the arm down is not the same as shutting the player down. That pitcher should still be in the weight room. Still at practice. Still playing a position, still hitting, still around their teammates. The rest is for the throwing arm, not for the athlete. A pitcher who disappears for three months comes back rusty, out of shape and outside the culture. A pitcher who trains all fall without throwing comes back stronger than they left.

High school players: your season starts now

If your player is in high school, this is the part to read twice.

School is back for most of the country and class schedules are still settling. This is the window where a counselor can still fix a schedule, and it closes fast. Make sure the classes on that schedule are the right ones, not just the ones that fit. For anyone with college ball in the picture, every core course grade this term goes straight onto the NCAA transcript, and the sliding scale does not care that it was a busy fall. The NCAA eligibility GPA guide lays out exactly which courses count.

Grades are the one thing that can close a door talent cannot reopen. I have watched it happen to better players than anyone reading this.

The other half is simpler. If your player wants a real role in the spring, this is when they prove they are serious, and the proof is boring and repeatable. Be early. Be in the weight room. Be the one who makes practice better for everybody else. Coaches remember the fall version of a player far more than they remember one good March afternoon.

And yes, this is also when the right eyes can be in the stands. But chasing that is backwards. Get genuinely better, be someone a coach wants in their program, and being seen takes care of itself.

Position by position, what this fall is for

Fall is not the same season for everybody on the roster. Here is roughly how I would use it.

  • Catchers. Fall is where you find out whether a player actually wants it, because the answer arrives around week three when the novelty is gone and the legs hurt. If your player is curious about catching, this is the season to try it. If they already catch, fall is for the parts nobody drills in spring: receiving, blocking with intent, and running an inning without being told what to do.
  • Pitchers. Either shut down completely, or if they are genuinely fresh, use the fall to build the pitch they do not have yet. Not to sharpen the one they already trust. Fall is the only time all year they can throw a bad changeup in a real game and have it cost nothing.
  • Middle infielders. If they are moving to a bigger diamond, the fall is that transition. Longer throws change footwork, arm slot and how early they have to decide. Better to be late on the exchange in October than in April.
  • Corner infielders and corner outfielders. The bat carries these spots and everybody knows it. Fall is a hitting block. Fewer at bats, but a real chance to change something mechanical without a slump costing anybody a game that matters.
  • The kid who is not sure where they fit. This is the most valuable fall project on any roster. Learn a second position. See the "I can put him anywhere" line above. That is a roster spot, and it is available to any player willing to be uncomfortable for eight weeks.

Younger players: use the one season where trying is free

For players roughly 8 to 12, the pressure is off in a way it will never quite be again, and that makes fall the best experimentation window of the year.

An infielder who has always wondered about catching can put the gear on in September without costing anybody a playoff spot. A player who has thrown one pitch their whole life can start learning a second one in games instead of only in the bullpen. A corner outfielder can find out whether they can handle center. None of that is realistic in April, when every inning has a standings implication.

And if your player is moving up an age group or onto a bigger field in the spring, fall is where that jump stops being scary. Being overmatched in October with nothing riding on it is a completely different experience than being overmatched in April with a roster spot on the line.

Give them one goal for the whole season. One. "Learn to catch." "Land the changeup in a game." "Stop bailing on the inside pitch." A season with one target beats a season with a vague hope of improvement every time.

How the eight weeks actually go

Fall seasons fail in a predictable place, and if you know where it is you can get your player through it.

Weeks one and two, the honeymoon. New team, new gear, everybody is excited. This is when you set the weight room habit and when your player should ask the coach for the position they want to try. Both windows close quietly. A short, polite ask in week one gets a yes. The same ask in week five gets "we will see."

Weeks three through five, where it is actually won. The novelty is gone, school has piled up, the weather turns, and the games do not matter. This is the exact stretch where most players quietly downshift, and it is the exact stretch coaches are watching hardest. Nobody is impressed by week one effort. Everybody notices week four effort. Tell your player that out loud, because if they know the test is coming they tend to pass it.

Weeks six through eight, apply it. Take the thing they have been drilling and use it in a game, in a real count, when it feels risky. Throw the changeup 2 and 0. Stay on the breaking ball instead of pulling off. A skill is not learned until it survives a game, and this is the last chance to fail at it for free.

This is where a program is actually built

There is one more thing fall does that no spring season can, and it does not show up in any statistic.

Fall is when a group of players becomes a team. The stakes are low, the schedule is loose, and there is actually time to talk to each other. The culture a program carries into a spring season is almost always poured in September and October, not March. New players find out who they are playing with. Returning players find out whether they are going to lead or just occupy a locker.

If you are a parent, protect that. The kid who skips the optional stuff to get one more private lesson in is not gaining what they think they are gaining.

What actually made the lab work

I have thought about this a lot since Saturday, because I would like every player I coach to get what I got.

The lab was not special because of the equipment. It was a facility, and there are nicer ones now on every other corner. What made it work was three things, and none of them cost money.

Everyone in there was working on something they were bad at. That was the culture. Nobody was in the cage performing. You went in to fix something, which meant failing in front of people all day long, which meant nobody was embarrassed to fail. That is the single hardest environment to create and the most valuable one there is.

The older guys taught the younger ones. Not because anybody assigned it. Because that is what you did. A kid got to watch someone four years ahead of them struggle with something, and suddenly their own struggle was normal instead of proof they were not good enough.

Nobody kept score. There was no record. No standings, no ranking, no leaderboard on a wall. The only measure was whether the thing you could not do in March you could do in June.

You can build a version of that for your player in a garage with a net and a bucket of balls. What you cannot build is the part where it is safe to be bad at something. That part is entirely up to the adults in the room, and it is decided by how you react the first time they fail in front of you.

Help them keep the receipts

Here is the practical problem with everything above: fall is short, and the details evaporate.

The week the squat went up. The at-bat where they finally stayed back on the breaking ball. The inning behind the plate that went badly and exactly why. The stretch in October where the arm felt tired and they said something instead of hiding it.

By February none of that is in anyone's memory, and all of it matters. It matters when a coach asks what they have been doing. It matters when a player is convinced they have not improved and the record says otherwise. It matters when you are sitting in a kitchen deciding about the next season and you would rather look at evidence than at the loudest voice in the group chat.

Fall is a clean page. Nothing carries over from spring, which makes this the easiest time your player will ever have starting the habit of writing things down. Five minutes after a workout or a game. What they did, how it felt, what happened.

That is the whole reason I built MyGrind. Not to make your player train more. To make sure the work they already do stops disappearing.

The games this fall will not be remembered. The work will show up in spring, when it actually counts.

Give your player eight weeks that are worth remembering, and then help them remember them. Give them a lab.
